# Finance, Accounting & Fundraising

## Capital Acquisition

**Pitch deck scripting:** A fundable deck is usually 10-12 slides in this order: (1) one-line what/why now, (2) problem, (3) solution, (4) market size (TAM/SAM/SOM with bottom-up math, not just a big TAM number), (5) product/demo, (6) business model — how it makes money, (7) traction (the strongest number you have — revenue, users, growth rate), (8) go-to-market, (9) competition (a real positioning map, not "we have no competitors"), (10) team, (11) financials/ask, (12) the ask itself (amount, use of funds, runway it buys). Investors skim — the traction and market size slides get the most scrutiny.

**VC vs. angel vs. crowdfunding:** VC fits high-growth, venture-scale businesses willing to trade significant equity/control for large capital and expects a path to a big exit. Angels are earlier, smaller checks, often faster and more founder-friendly, good for pre-traction or pre-seed. Crowdfunding (reward-based like Kickstarter, or equity like Wefunder/StartEngine) fits consumer products with a story people want to back publicly, and doubles as a demand-validation and marketing channel. Match the fundraising path to the business's actual growth ceiling — not every business should raise VC.

**Cap table management:** Track fully diluted ownership (including the option pool) at every raise, not just before/after the new round. Common early mistake: founders forget the option pool gets created/expanded *before* the new investor's money comes in, which dilutes existing holders more than expected — model this explicitly when discussing a raise.

## Financial Modeling

- **Cash flow forecasting:** Build 13-week rolling cash flow for operational management (short-term, high-detail) and a 12-18 month model for planning/fundraising. Always separate cash from revenue — a profitable business can still run out of cash from timing mismatches (e.g. paying suppliers before customers pay invoices).
- **Burn rate & runway:** Gross burn = total monthly cash out. Net burn = cash out minus cash in. Runway = current cash balance ÷ net burn. Always state runway in months and flag it clearly if it's under 6 months — that's a "raise now or cut costs now" signal, not a someday problem.
- **Break-even analysis:** Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ (price − variable cost per unit). Push the user for real numbers here rather than round estimates — break-even is only useful if the inputs are accurate.
- **Cap table management:** See above under Capital Acquisition.

## Managerial Accounting

- **Budgeting:** Zero-based (justify every line each period) for early-stage/cash-constrained businesses; incremental (prior period + adjustments) is fine once the business is stable and predictable.
- **COGS tracking:** For physical products, COGS = materials + direct labor + manufacturing overhead + shipping/freight to get the product ready for sale. For services, COGS is often just the direct labor/subcontractor cost of delivery. Gross margin = (Revenue − COGS) / Revenue — track this per product/service line, not just in aggregate, to find which offerings are actually profitable.
- **Variance analysis:** Compare actual vs. budget monthly; investigate any line off by more than ~10-15%, in either direction (overperformance can reveal a forecasting error just as much as underperformance reveals a problem).
- **Tax preparation frameworks:** Keep a running log of deductible categories relevant to the business type (home office, equipment, mileage, contractor payments, R&D credit if applicable). Always caveat that tax specifics require a CPA — this skill can organize the framework, not file the return.

## Pricing & Monetization

- **Value-based pricing:** Price against the quantifiable value/outcome delivered to the customer (time saved, revenue generated, cost avoided), not against cost-plus or "what competitors charge." Ask: what would this customer pay to *not* have this problem?
- **Subscription models:** Anchor on the metric that scales with customer value (seats, usage volume, revenue) — avoid pure flat-rate pricing once there's meaningful variance in customer size.
- **Freemium mechanics:** The free tier should be genuinely useful (to drive word-of-mouth and habit formation) but hit a real limitation tied to value delivered (usage cap, feature gate, team size) that naturally motivates upgrading — not an arbitrary annoyance.
- **Tier structuring:** 3 tiers is the common sweet spot (a "good/better/best" structure anchors buyers toward the middle). Name tiers by outcome or persona, not just "Basic/Pro/Enterprise" when a more specific name would clarify who each tier is for.
