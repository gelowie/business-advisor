# Strategy & Corporate Governance

## Market Entry & Growth

**Go-to-market (GTM):** A GTM plan should always answer five things concretely: (1) who exactly is the first customer (a named segment, not "everyone"), (2) what channel reaches them first, (3) what's the message/hook, (4) what does success look like in 90 days, (5) what's the kill criteria if it doesn't work. Avoid GTM plans that list every possible channel — pick 1-2 to go deep on first.

**International expansion:** Sequence by (a) regulatory/legal complexity, (b) existing demand signals (inbound traffic/inquiries from that market), (c) localization cost (language, currency, payment methods, cultural fit). Recommend piloting with a lightweight entry (partner/distributor, digital-only) before committing to physical presence (entity, hires, office).

**Competitive moat building:** Real moats are usually one of: switching costs, network effects, brand/trust, proprietary data, economies of scale, or regulatory/licensing barriers. "We work harder" or "better customer service" are not moats — they're table stakes. When advising, name which moat category applies and how to deepen it.

## Strategic Frameworks

- **Blue Ocean Strategy:** Use the Four Actions Framework — what to Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, and Create relative to the industry's current competitive factors — to find uncontested market space rather than out-competing on the same dimensions everyone else does.
- **Porter's Five Forces:** Assess (1) threat of new entrants, (2) bargaining power of suppliers, (3) bargaining power of buyers, (4) threat of substitutes, (5) rivalry among existing competitors. Rate each High/Medium/Low with a one-line reason — this is a diagnostic, not just a list.
- **SWOT:** Don't stop at four lists. For every point, add "so what" (strategic implication) and "now what" (a specific action). See the SWOT template in `assets/templates/Strategy and Governance/SWOT Analysis and Action Plan.txt`.
- **PESTLE:** Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental — use for macro-environment scans before market entry or major pivots, not for routine internal decisions.
- **OKRs:** Objectives are qualitative and inspiring ("Become the fastest-growing player in X"); Key Results are 2-4 measurable numbers per objective ("Grow MRR from $20k to $50k"). A common failure mode is writing KRs that are actually just tasks ("Launch feature X") — push back on those and ask "launching X moves what number, by how much?"

## Corporate Structure

- **Entity selection:** LLC for simplicity and pass-through taxation, best for most small/service businesses. C-Corp (often Delaware) when raising venture capital, issuing stock options, or planning an eventual IPO/acquisition — VCs generally require it. S-Corp for tax efficiency on owner salary once profit is significant. B-Corp/PBC when the mission/impact commitment is core to the brand and the founder wants legal protection for balancing profit with other stakeholders. Always caveat: this is a starting framework, not legal/tax advice — a lawyer or CPA should confirm before filing.
- **Board of directors:** Required for C-Corps, optional for LLCs. Early-stage boards are typically 3-5 people: founder(s), 1-2 investor seats (if funded), 1-2 independents chosen for specific expertise gaps (e.g., a former operator in the target industry).
- **Advisory boards:** Lower commitment than a formal board — no fiduciary duty, no equity required (though small equity grants, 0.1-0.5%, or a flat fee are common). Use for specific expertise gaps (technical, industry access, fundraising) rather than as a vanity credential list.

## Risk Management

- **Crisis management planning:** Identify the 3-5 most plausible crisis scenarios for this specific business (not generic ones) — e.g., for a SaaS: major outage or data breach; for a restaurant: health code violation or bad viral review; for e-commerce: supply chain failure. For each, pre-define: who makes the call, what the first external communication says, and the internal escalation path.
- **Business continuity:** What's the single point of failure that would stop the business cold (one supplier, one key employee, one platform dependency)? Continuity planning starts by naming that, then reducing dependency or building a documented backup plan.
- **Regulatory compliance tracking:** Maintain a simple living list — regulation/requirement, renewal/review frequency, owner, last checked date. Most compliance failures are not "we didn't know the rule" but "we forgot to renew/recheck."
