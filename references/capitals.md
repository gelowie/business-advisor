# The Six Capitals — what a business actually runs on

Money is only one stock of value. A business also depends on equipment, knowledge, people, relationships and the natural environment. These are the six capitals used in integrated reporting. Financial problems often *originate* in one of the other five — a margin collapse can be a human-capital problem (turnover), a sales stall can be a social-capital problem (lost referrals), a cost spike can be a natural-capital problem (water, energy, materials).

**Honesty rule:** standard accounting records almost none of the non-financial capitals (self-built brand, know-how, relationships, culture, goodwill from reputation do not appear on the balance sheet). So **never assign a precise dollar value to them.** Use measurable proxies, ranges and trends, and label every estimate as an estimate.

## The six capitals

### 1. Financial capital — the funds available
Cash, credit access, retained earnings, invested funds. Measure with the statements and ratios in `financial-core.md`. Failure signal: runway under ~6 months, covenant pressure, dependence on one lender or investor. Converts to: the ability to buy, hire, survive a bad quarter.

### 2. Manufactured (constructed) capital — physical means of production
Equipment, buildings, fit-out, vehicles, tooling, software infrastructure, inventory-handling systems. Measure: utilization %, age vs useful life, downtime hours, maintenance cost trend, replacement cost, revenue per unit of asset. Failure signal: rising repair cost, capacity bottleneck, idle assets, deferred maintenance. Converts to: output capacity, quality, unit cost.

### 3. Intellectual capital — knowledge the business owns or controls
IP (trademarks, copyrights, patents, trade secrets), proprietary data, processes and SOPs, software, brand strength, licenses, documented know-how. Measure: IP register completeness, share of operations documented as SOPs, brand-search volume or direct-traffic share, repeat-by-brand purchase rate, % of know-how held by one person. Failure signal: critical knowledge lives in one head, unregistered or unprotected IP, no documentation, brand dependent on the founder. Converts to: pricing power, lower training cost, scalability, saleability.

### 4. Human capital — skills, health, motivation and capacity of people
Staff and owner: capability, experience, engagement, retention. Measure: voluntary turnover %, time-to-hire and time-to-productivity, revenue (or gross profit) per employee, training hours and spend, absenteeism, key-person dependency, owner hours worked per week, internal promotion rate. Failure signal: rising turnover, overtime dependence, owner as bottleneck, unfilled critical roles. Converts to: output quality, speed, customer experience, recruiting cost (replacing an employee commonly costs a large fraction of annual pay — estimate for the specific role rather than quoting a generic figure).

### 5. Social and relationship capital — trust and networks
Customers, suppliers, partners, community, regulators, reputation. Measure: repeat-purchase rate, net promoter score or review trend, share of new customers from referrals, customer concentration, supplier terms and reliability, partner count and activity, community standing (local press, permits without friction). Failure signal: falling referrals, deteriorating supplier terms, single-customer dependence, rising complaints, strained landlord or regulator relationship. Converts to: lower acquisition cost, better credit terms, resilience in a downturn.

### 6. Natural capital — environmental inputs and effects
Energy, water, raw materials, land, climate exposure, waste and emissions. Measure: energy and water cost per unit of output, waste % and disposal cost, material yield, supply exposure to weather or resource scarcity, regulatory exposure (emissions, discharge, packaging rules). Failure signal: input-cost volatility, supply disruption from climate events, rising compliance or disposal costs. Converts to: input cost, supply continuity, license to operate, customer and investor preference.

## How capitals interact

- **Trade-offs are the point.** Squeezing human capital (overwork) lifts this quarter's financial capital and erodes next year's. Deferring maintenance does the same to manufactured capital. State which capital is being spent to produce a result.
- **Conversion chains.** Human → intellectual (training and documentation become SOPs) → social (consistent service builds trust) → financial (repeat revenue). Break one link and the chain's financial output drops.
- **A leak in one capital can look like a problem in another.** Always trace the symptom to its source before prescribing a fix.

## Weighting by business type

| Type | Capitals that usually matter most | Typical hidden weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Service-based | Human, social, intellectual | Revenue tied to the owner's personal relationships and hours |
| Digital / SaaS | Intellectual, human, financial | Know-how concentrated in founders; heavy burn on acquisition |
| Physical / e-commerce | Manufactured, financial, natural, social (suppliers) | Cash trapped in stock; single-supplier dependence |
| Local brick-and-mortar | Social (community), human, manufactured | Key staff or one landlord relationship; thin cash buffer |

## Using the lens in practice

1. For a diagnosis, rate each capital as **strong / adequate / weak / unknown** using at least one proxy from the lists above, and say what evidence the rating rests on.
2. Identify the capital whose weakness best explains the financial symptom, and the evidence for and against.
3. Recommend actions that rebuild that capital, with a measurable target and a timeframe, and name what it will cost in money and in other capitals.
4. Never claim a dollar value for a non-financial capital; offer an estimated range with its assumptions if the user needs one (e.g. cost to replace a key employee, cost of re-documenting a process).
