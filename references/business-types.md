# Business Type Lenses

Read this file whenever you need to translate a domain framework (from the other reference files) into advice that actually fits the user's business. The four types below aren't just "examples" — each one changes what "good" looks like across all eight domains.

## 1. Service-Based

**Examples:** Marketing/design agencies, consulting firms, freelancers, accounting, legal services, coaching.

**What's different:** Revenue comes from time/expertise, not units. The core assets are reputation, client relationships, and utilization rate. Growth is bottlenecked by hours in the day until you build a team or productize.

How this reframes each domain:
- **Strategy:** Moat = expertise + relationships + case studies, not IP or scale. Entity/board questions are usually simpler (LLC/S-Corp, no board needed until much later).
- **Finance:** Track utilization rate (billable hours / available hours) and realization rate (billed vs. collected) alongside cash flow. Pricing is the central lever — hourly vs. project vs. retainer vs. value-based.
- **Product:** The "product" is the service delivery process itself — productizing a service (fixed-scope packages) is the main innovation lever.
- **Marketing:** Referrals, case studies, and thought leadership (content that proves expertise) outperform paid acquisition for most service firms.
- **Sales:** Discovery calls, proposals, and retainer structuring are the whole game. Objection handling centers on price and "why you vs. a bigger firm."
- **Operations:** SOPs for client onboarding/offboarding, time tracking, and handoffs between team members. Capacity planning replaces inventory planning.
- **HR:** Hiring is hiring judgment and client-facing skill, not just execution. Retention matters enormously because client relationships live with individual people.
- **Legal:** MSAs, SOWs, and scope-creep protection are the most-used documents. IP assignment clauses (who owns the deliverable) matter a lot.

## 2. Digital Product & Tech (SaaS/Software)

**Examples:** SaaS, mobile apps, online courses, digital templates, newsletters.

**What's different:** Near-zero marginal cost per unit, but high upfront build cost and constant iteration pressure. Success is measured in recurring metrics, not one-off sales.

How this reframes each domain:
- **Strategy:** Moat = switching costs, network effects, data, or brand — not physical assets. GTM is often product-led (free trial/freemium) or sales-led (enterprise) depending on price point.
- **Finance:** CAC, LTV, LTV:CAC ratio (aim for 3:1+), churn/retention, MRR/ARR, and burn multiple matter more than traditional COGS. Fundraising narratives lean on growth rate and retention curves.
- **Product:** This is the center of gravity. MVP scoping, roadmap prioritization (RICE/ICE), PMF signals (Sean Ellis test, retention curves), and Agile/Scrum cadence are daily-use frameworks.
- **Marketing:** CAC payback period, funnel conversion at each step (signup → activation → paid), content/SEO for organic acquisition, in-app onboarding as a marketing surface.
- **Sales:** Self-serve funnels for low-ACV products; structured B2B sales motion (demo → trial → close) for higher ACV. Lead scoring based on product usage signals ("product-qualified leads").
- **Operations:** Uptime/reliability SLAs, incident response, deployment process, and support ticket triage replace physical logistics.
- **HR:** Engineering/product hiring bar, remote-friendly structures, equity as part of comp are common considerations.
- **Legal:** Terms of Service, Privacy Policy, data processing agreements, and IP (who owns the code, especially with contractors) are the priority documents. GDPR/CCPA compliance is often non-optional from day one if there's any EU/CA user base.

## 3. Physical Product (E-commerce & Retail)

**Examples:** Shopify stores, Amazon FBA, local boutiques, manufacturing brands, dropshipping.

**What's different:** Real inventory, real logistics, real unit economics with tangible COGS. Cash gets tied up in stock. Margins are thinner and more fragile than digital.

How this reframes each domain:
- **Strategy:** Moat = brand, sourcing relationships, distribution access, or proprietary product design. Market entry decisions include channel strategy (own site vs. Amazon vs. wholesale vs. retail).
- **Finance:** COGS, gross margin per SKU, inventory carrying cost, and cash conversion cycle (how long cash is tied up in inventory before it becomes revenue again) are the critical numbers. Break-even must account for landed cost (product + shipping + duties + fees).
- **Product:** "Product development" means physical design, sourcing/manufacturing, sampling, and quality control — not sprints. Packaging is a marketing and unboxing-experience decision, not just protection.
- **Marketing:** Paid social/search for cold traffic, email/SMS for retention, influencer/UGC for social proof. CAC must be compared against contribution margin per order, not just revenue.
- **Sales:** Often channel-driven (marketplace algorithms, retail buyers, wholesale reps) rather than a traditional sales team. Amazon/marketplace optimization (listings, reviews, PPC) is its own sales discipline.
- **Operations:** Vendor/supplier selection, MOQs (minimum order quantities), freight/customs, 3PL vs. in-house fulfillment, inventory forecasting to avoid stockouts or overstock — this domain does the most work here.
- **HR:** Warehouse/fulfillment staffing, seasonal scaling (peak season hiring), quality control roles.
- **Legal:** Product liability, safety compliance/certifications, supplier contracts, trademark on brand and packaging design, import/export compliance.

## 4. Local Brick-and-Mortar

**Examples:** Cafes, gyms, salons, dental offices, repair shops, restaurants.

**What's different:** Growth is geographically bounded. Success depends on foot traffic, local reputation, and physical capacity (seats, chairs, appointment slots) rather than scale economics.

How this reframes each domain:
- **Strategy:** Moat = location, local reputation, and loyal repeat customers. "Market entry" usually means site selection and local competitive density, not TAM/SAM/SOM math.
- **Finance:** Revenue per square foot, average ticket size, table/chair/slot turnover rate, and labor cost as % of revenue are the key ratios. Rent is a fixed cost that dominates the break-even calculation.
- **Product:** "Product" is the in-location experience — menu/service menu design, ambiance, wait time, staff-customer interaction.
- **Marketing:** Google Business Profile optimization, local SEO, local reviews (Yelp/Google), community partnerships and local events, foot-traffic-driving promotions (not broad digital funnels).
- **Sales:** Walk-in conversion, appointment booking systems, upsell at point of service, membership/loyalty programs for repeat visits.
- **Operations:** Shift scheduling, opening/closing SOPs, health/safety compliance, point-of-sale and queue management, local supplier relationships.
- **HR:** Hourly staff scheduling and turnover (often high in this segment), local labor law compliance (minimum wage, scheduling laws), tip/service-charge policy.
- **Legal:** Local business licensing/permits, health department compliance, lease negotiation, liability insurance, ADA/accessibility compliance for the physical space.

## Using this file

When a user's request maps cleanly onto one of these four types, apply that section's reframing on top of whatever domain framework you're using from the other reference files. If a business genuinely straddles two types (e.g. a boutique that sells physical goods online and in a physical shop), say so and blend the relevant sections rather than forcing a single lens.
